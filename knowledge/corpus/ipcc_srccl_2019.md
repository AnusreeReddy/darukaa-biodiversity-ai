# IPCC Special Report on Climate Change and Land (SRCCL), 2019

## Drylands and desertification
Drylands are defined using the aridity index, and are divided into hyper-arid, arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid zones. Dryland populations are highly vulnerable to desertification and climate change. In dryland areas, climate change and desertification interact: warming increases evaporative demand while rainfall becomes more variable, so the same annual rainfall total supports less plant production than it did historically.

## Land degradation
Land degradation reduces the productivity of land, and affects a substantial share of the ice-free land surface. Soil erosion from agricultural fields is estimated to be substantially greater than soil formation rates, meaning that on conventionally tilled cropland soil is being lost far faster than it is being replaced. Reversing this requires maintaining continuous ground cover rather than only reducing tillage intensity.

## Response options assessed
The report assesses land management response options including increased soil organic carbon content, reduced soil erosion, agroforestry, improved cropland management, improved grazing land management, fire management, reduced deforestation and degradation, restoration of degraded land, and afforestation and reforestation.

Several of these options deliver co-benefits across mitigation, adaptation, desertification control, land degradation and food security simultaneously. Increased soil organic carbon content, agroforestry, and reduced deforestation and degradation are identified among the options with co-benefits across multiple objectives and limited adverse side effects when deployed at appropriate scales.

## Why soil organic carbon is central
Soil organic matter influences water holding capacity, nutrient supply and soil structure. Increasing soil organic carbon therefore simultaneously supports climate mitigation and adaptation to drought, which is why it is repeatedly identified as a response option with co-benefits rather than trade-offs.

## Scale caution
The report emphasises that response options which require large areas of land, such as large-scale afforestation or bioenergy, can have adverse side effects on food security and biodiversity if deployed at very large scale, whereas options integrated into existing land use, such as agroforestry and improved cropland management, avoid this competition.

## Temperature and organic matter
Warming accelerates the decomposition of soil organic matter. In warm and hot climates, a given quantity of added biomass carbon is mineralised more rapidly than in cool climates, so maintaining a raised soil carbon stock requires sustained annual inputs rather than a single intervention.
