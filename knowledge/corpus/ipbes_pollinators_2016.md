# IPBES Assessment Report on Pollinators, Pollination and Food Production (2016)

## Dependence of production on pollinators
A large share of global food crop types benefit to some degree from animal pollination, and pollinator-dependent crops account for a disproportionate share of the volume and nutritional value of global food production, particularly fruits, vegetables, nuts and seeds. Staple cereals such as wheat, rice and maize are wind- or self-pollinated and do not depend on animal pollination, so pollinator decline affects dietary diversity and farm income more than staple calorie supply.

## Status and drivers
Wild pollinators have declined in occurrence and diversity at local and regional scales in north-west Europe and North America, and a substantial fraction of assessed wild bee and butterfly species are threatened at regional level. The assessment identifies the principal drivers as land-use change and habitat loss, intensive agricultural management, pesticide use, invasive alien species, pathogens and climate change, acting in combination rather than singly.

## Pesticides
Pesticides, particularly insecticides, have been shown to have lethal and sub-lethal effects on pollinators under field-realistic conditions. Sub-lethal effects include impaired foraging behaviour, navigation and reproduction, which reduce colony and population performance without producing visible mortality. Herbicide use affects pollinators indirectly by removing the flowering weeds that supply forage within and around crop fields. Consequently, chemical pressure can suppress pollinator populations even where floral habitat has been provided, and reducing exposure is a necessary complement to habitat creation rather than an alternative to it.

## Effective responses
Responses identified as effective include increasing the diversity and continuity of floral resources through the season, maintaining and restoring semi-natural habitat in and around farmland, reducing exposure to pesticides through integrated pest management and application timing, and supporting diversified farming systems. Continuity of forage matters as much as total quantity: a landscape with a short intense bloom and a long resource gap supports fewer pollinators than one with lower peak abundance but continuous flowering.

## Nesting resources
Floral resources alone are insufficient. Many wild bee species nest in bare ground, dead wood or hollow stems, and the availability of these nesting substrates frequently limits populations independently of forage supply. Interventions that supply flowers but eliminate undisturbed nesting substrate deliver less than expected.
