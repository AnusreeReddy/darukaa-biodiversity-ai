# FAO - Recarbonizing global soils: a technical manual of recommended management practices (2021)

## Purpose
The manual compiles sustainable soil management practices that maintain or increase soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks, with practice descriptions, expected effects and implementation constraints across cropland, grassland and degraded land.

## Core practice families
Practices that raise SOC act through only two levers: increasing organic carbon input to the soil, or decreasing its rate of loss. Practices include retention of crop residues, cover cropping and green manures, organic amendments such as compost and manure, diversified rotations including legumes, reduced or zero tillage, agroforestry and tree integration, improved grazing management, and the restoration of degraded and eroded land.

## Legumes and nitrogen
Including legumes in rotations or as cover crops introduces biologically fixed nitrogen. Because carbon and nitrogen accumulate in soil organic matter at a relatively constrained ratio, nitrogen supply frequently limits the conversion of added carbon into stabilised organic matter. Legume inclusion therefore raises the efficiency with which added biomass becomes durable soil organic carbon, in addition to reducing dependence on synthetic nitrogen fertiliser. Effective nodulation requires adequate soil pH; on strongly acidic soils, rhizobial symbiosis is impaired and expected nitrogen fixation will not be realised until pH is corrected.

## Residue retention and soil cover
Maintaining continuous soil cover reduces raindrop impact, surface sealing and erosion losses, lowers soil surface temperature and reduces evaporative loss of soil water. In warm and dry environments the water conservation effect of surface residue is frequently the dominant short-term benefit, preceding any measurable change in SOC stock.

## Dryland constraints
In arid and semi-arid systems the quantity of biomass that can be produced is itself water-limited, which caps the rate of carbon input achievable. Practices in drylands should therefore prioritise water capture and retention first, because the resulting biomass increase is what subsequently drives carbon accumulation. Water harvesting structures, contour measures, mulching and planting pits are used to convert unproductive runoff into stored soil water.

## Permanence and reversibility
SOC gains are reversible. A soil that has accumulated carbon over a decade can lose a large share of that gain within a few seasons if tillage is resumed, residues removed or vegetation cover lost. SOC-based interventions require continuity of management to retain their benefit.

## Measurement guidance
Detecting a change in SOC against high spatial background variability typically requires several years and a consistent sampling protocol with fixed depth increments and bulk density correction. Short-term monitoring should therefore track proxy indicators that respond faster, such as aggregate stability, infiltration rate, earthworm counts or mineralisable carbon.
