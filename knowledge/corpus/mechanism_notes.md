# Compiled mechanism notes: how environmental variables interact

This document is a compiled synthesis of mechanisms described across the indexed source documents. It contains no new empirical claims and is used by the reasoning engine to explain causal links between variables. Quantified effects always come from the primary sources, not from this note.

## Soil organic carbon and plant-available water
Organic matter increases plant-available water capacity through two routes: it holds water directly, and more importantly it promotes aggregation, which creates the mid-sized pores that retain water against gravity while remaining accessible to roots. The consequence is that raising soil organic carbon partially substitutes for rainfall. On a soil with depleted organic carbon, a larger fraction of each rainfall event is lost to runoff and evaporation rather than stored. This is why soil carbon interventions and water availability interventions in dry regions are not separate agendas: the same change addresses both, and the effect on species survival runs through moisture persistence during dry spells.

## Soil organic carbon and soil biodiversity
Soil organic matter is the energy substrate for the soil food web. Microbial biomass, fungal networks and soil fauna are limited by carbon supply, so depleted soils support reduced and less diverse soil communities. Because those organisms mediate nutrient mineralisation and aggregate formation, the relationship is bidirectional and self-reinforcing in both directions: carbon loss reduces biology, and reduced biology slows the stabilisation of new carbon.

## Soil pH as a gate on other interventions
Soil pH does not usually limit biodiversity directly. It acts as a gate: below roughly pH 5.5, aluminium solubility restricts root growth, phosphorus is fixed, and rhizobial symbiosis is impaired. Any recommendation that depends on legume nitrogen fixation or on deep rooting will therefore under-deliver on strongly acidic soil until pH is corrected. PH must be checked before legume-based interventions are recommended, not after.

## Structural habitat, temperature and refugia
Vegetation structure changes the microclimate at the scale organisms actually experience. Canopy and litter layers reduce peak surface temperature and evaporative demand. In hot climates this converts structural habitat interventions into thermal refugia, which is a distinct benefit from the food-resource benefit of the same vegetation.

## Habitat area, connectivity and fragmentation
Species richness responds to habitat area, to habitat heterogeneity and to connectivity as three partly independent variables. Adding area without connectivity produces isolated patches subject to local extinction without recolonisation. Adding heterogeneity without sufficient area produces many habitat types none of which is large enough to support a viable population. Where deforestation has isolated remnants, linear features such as hedgerows, riparian strips and tree lines address connectivity specifically, and are not substitutes for patch area.

## Pollution as a capping constraint
Chemical pressure acts multiplicatively rather than additively with habitat quality. Habitat created within a heavily treated matrix can function as an ecological trap, attracting organisms into an area of high exposure. Where pollution is high, reducing exposure is a precondition for habitat interventions to deliver their expected effect.

## Temperature and the permanence of carbon gains
Decomposition rate rises with temperature. In warm and hot climates a larger fraction of added organic input is mineralised each year, so a given practice yields a smaller net stock change and requires continuing input to hold it. Cool climates retain gains more readily. The same intervention therefore carries different expected magnitudes and different permanence risk depending on the thermal regime.

## Water availability as the binding constraint in drylands
Where rainfall is the limiting factor, biomass production is capped by water. Since carbon input depends on biomass, carbon interventions in drylands are indirectly water-limited. The practical ordering is therefore to capture and retain water first, allow biomass to increase, and let carbon accumulation follow, rather than attempting to raise carbon inputs directly against a water ceiling.
