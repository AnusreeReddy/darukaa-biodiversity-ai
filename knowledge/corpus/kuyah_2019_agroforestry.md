# Agroforestry delivers a win-win solution for ecosystem services in sub-Saharan Africa: a meta-analysis

## Scope
A meta-analysis of agroforestry practices across ecological conditions in sub-Saharan Africa, drawing on over 1,100 observations comparing agroforestry with non-agroforestry controls.

## Findings
Agroforestry practices significantly increased crop yield, soil organic carbon and total soil nitrogen, while decreasing runoff and soil loss, compared with non-agroforestry practices. The simultaneous improvement in production and in soil and water regulation outcomes is the basis for describing the result as a win-win rather than a trade-off.

## Mechanisms
Trees in cropland contribute organic carbon through leaf litter, root turnover and pruning residues, which is the input term driving soil organic carbon accumulation. Nitrogen-fixing tree species additionally raise soil nitrogen supply. Canopy and litter cover intercept rainfall energy and slow overland flow, which is what reduces runoff and soil loss. Reduced runoff increases the fraction of rainfall that infiltrates and is stored in the profile, which is particularly consequential where rainfall is low and erratic.

## Context dependence
The magnitude of benefit varies with species selection, tree density, arrangement and management. Poorly matched species or excessive tree density can compete with the crop for water and light, and in water-limited environments this competition is the principal risk to be managed. Tree arrangement, pruning regime and species choice are therefore not implementation details but determinants of whether the intervention delivers net benefit.

## Arid zone response
Complementary global meta-analytic work reports that soil organic carbon responses to agroforestry are largest in arid zones, and that agroforestry raises soil microbial biomass and diversity and soil nutrient content relative to monoculture. The interpretation is that in the most water- and carbon-limited systems, the microclimate and input changes introduced by trees produce the greatest proportional change, while absolute stocks remain modest.
