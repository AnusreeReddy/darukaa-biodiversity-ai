# The effectiveness of flower strips and hedgerows on pest control, pollination services and crop yield: a quantitative synthesis

## Scope
A quantitative synthesis of field studies testing whether sown flower strips and hedgerows adjacent to crop fields enhance pest control, crop pollination and yield in the adjacent crop.

## Pest control
Flower strips enhanced pest control services in adjacent fields by 16% on average. Hedgerows did not show a significant effect on pest control in this synthesis, indicating that herbaceous floral resources and woody linear features act through different mechanisms and on different timescales.

## Pollination and yield
Effects on crop pollination and yield were considerably more variable than pest control effects. Two important drivers of that variability were identified. First, pollination services declined exponentially with distance from the planting, so the benefit is concentrated in the field margin and decays rapidly inward. Strip placement and field geometry therefore determine how much of a field actually receives the service. Second, perennial and older flower strips with higher flowering plant diversity enhanced pollination more effectively than young or species-poor strips. Benefits accrue over years as the sward matures, not in the establishment season.

## Design implications
Because the effect decays with distance, narrow fields or fields with multiple internal strips capture proportionally more benefit than a single perimeter strip around a large block. Because effects strengthen with age and floral diversity, a species-rich perennial mix maintained over multiple years substantially outperforms an annual monofloral sowing.

## Related evidence on pollinator communities
Separate meta-analytic work on field-margin floral enhancements found consistent increases in pollinator abundance and richness at the field edge itself, but no consistent spillover of pollinators into the crop interior. This distinction matters: field margin plantings are reliably effective as a pollinator *conservation* measure, while their effect on in-field *pollination service* is conditional on distance, strip age and landscape context.

## Landscape context
Evidence on semi-natural vegetation indicates that the positive effects of diversification on biodiversity strengthen once semi-natural vegetation exceeds roughly 10% of the landscape and are strongest up to about 20%, consistent with the intermediate landscape complexity hypothesis. In landscapes that are already structurally complex, adding another strip yields a smaller marginal gain than in a simplified landscape that nonetheless retains some source habitat.
